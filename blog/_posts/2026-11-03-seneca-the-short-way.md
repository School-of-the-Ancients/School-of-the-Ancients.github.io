Sometime between 62 and 65 CE, in the last years of his life, the Roman philosopher Seneca was writing letters to a friend named Lucilius. Those letters are now one of the best records we have of how the Stoics thought about teaching.

In Letter 6, Lucilius asks Seneca to share what's been helping him. Seneca says yes. He'll send the actual books, and he'll even mark the good passages so Lucilius doesn't waste time hunting for them. Then he adds a "but":

> "Of course, however, the living voice and the intimacy of a common life will help you more than the written word. You must go to the scene of action, first, because men put more faith in their eyes than in their ears, and second, because the way is long if one follows precepts, but short and helpful, if one follows patterns."

In Latin that last part is *longum iter est per praecepta, breve et efficax per exempla*. Rules are the long way, and examples are the short one.

I keep coming back to this letter because it describes, pretty exactly, the problem with most AI in education right now.

## Rules versus patterns

A precept is a rule you can write down. "Don't get angry." "Check your sources." "Read carefully." They're true and they're easy to agree with, and they hardly ever change what anyone does.

A pattern (*exemplum*) is a person doing the thing. You watch them stay calm when someone insults them, or check a claim before repeating it, or slow down on a hard paragraph. Then you have something to copy.

Seneca backs this up with examples, which is a nice touch. He says Cleanthes couldn't have become so much like his teacher Zeno "if he had merely heard his lectures; he shared in his life, saw into his hidden purposes, and watched him to see whether he lived according to his own rules." Plato and Aristotle, he says, "derived more benefit from the character than from the words of Socrates." And it wasn't Epicurus's classroom that shaped his best students. It was "living together under the same roof."

Look at the test Cleanthes runs. He's checking whether Zeno lives by his own rules. That isn't passive watching. It's the kind of thing a scientist does: you have a claim, and you look at the evidence.

## A Roman emperor's thank-you list

About a century later, Marcus Aurelius opened his private notebook, the one we now call the *Meditations*, with a whole book of thank-yous. It's a list of people and what he learned from each of them.

What's striking is how few of the entries are facts or doctrines. Most of them are behaviors he saw. From his Stoic teacher Rusticus he learned "to read with diligence; not to rest satisfied with a light and superficial knowledge, nor quickly to assent to things commonly spoken of." From Sextus he took "his conversation being an example how a man might accommodate himself to all men and companies." (Both lines are from Meric Casaubon's 1634 translation.)

That's Seneca's point from the student's side. When Marcus wrote down what his education actually gave him, he mostly listed people and how they acted.

## Why this matters for AI

Most AI tutors today are precept machines. You type a question and a paragraph of correct-sounding rules comes back. That's useful the way Seneca's marked-up books were useful, but by his own standard it's the long way.

Here's the honest catch: an AI can't share its life with you. Cleanthes could watch whether Zeno kept his own rules over years, and no chatbot gives you that. So I don't think the lesson is "make the AI pretend to be a sage." What I take from it is narrower:

1. **Show, don't just tell.** Seneca says people trust their eyes more than their ears. (His translator points out that this echoes a line in Herodotus 1.8: "men trust their ears less than their eyes.") A wall of text is all ears.
2. **Do it where the learner is.** Seneca's word is *in rem praesentem*: go to the scene of action. The learning should happen around the thing itself, not in a separate box.
3. **Make the learner test it.** Cleanthes was checking his teacher against the evidence, and a good student should get to do the same.

And: "We're designing every lesson around one loop: learn it, try it yourself, reflect, and explain it back in your own words."

Seneca ends the letter by saying he's calling Lucilius over "not merely that you may derive benefit, but that you may confer benefit; for we can assist each other greatly." In Letter 7 he puts it more simply: "men learn while they teach." That's the explain-it-back step, written down almost 2,000 years before we needed it.
