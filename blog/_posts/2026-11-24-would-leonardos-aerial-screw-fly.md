This one is a lesson, so same deal as last time: a question first, then your guess, then the explanation.

## The question

In one of his notebooks, Leonardo da Vinci drew a big spiral of linen wrapped around a vertical pole, standing on a round platform. It looks like a corkscrew made of sailcloth. Next to it he wrote this (in Edward MacCurdy's translation):

> "I find that if this instrument made with a screw be well made — that is to say, made of linen of which the pores are stopped up with starch — and be turned swiftly, the said screw will make its spiral in the air and it will rise high."

He also gave it a size: "from the circumference to the centre let it be eight braccia." The Museo Scienza in Milan, which has a model of it, puts eight Florentine braccia at about 5 meters. This was not a toy. He meant a big machine.

So here's the question. Say you built it exactly as drawn, at full size, in Leonardo's Milan. Would it fly?

Take a second and actually commit to an answer before you scroll.

## Your guess

If you said yes, I get it. It looks like a helicopter. Helicopters fly. Leonardo was Leonardo.

If you said no, can you say *why*? "It's old" isn't a reason. What specifically goes wrong?

## The explanation

The short answer is no. The full-size machine wouldn't get off the ground. But the idea itself wasn't silly, and that's what makes it a good lesson.

**The idea is sound.** Leonardo was thinking of air as something you can push against, the way a screw pushes into wood or water. He even gives a way to feel it yourself in the same note: "Take the example of a wide and thin ruler whirled very rapidly in the air, you will see that your arm will be guided by the line of the edge of the said flat surface." Try it with a ruler or a piece of cardboard. The air pushes back. Spin a surface fast enough and that push can be turned into lift. That's roughly what a rotor does.

So where does it break? Two places.

**1. Nothing could turn it fast enough.** A screw that size has to spin hard to lift itself, plus its frame, plus whoever is turning it. In 1480s Milan there was no engine. The only power source was people. Engineer Jonathan Ridley of Solent University, writing in The Conversation in 2019, reads the drawing as showing four levers for people to push, and says "it's impossible for four people to produce anywhere near enough power for flight in this manner." The U.S. Centennial of Flight Commission's history of early helicopters says the same thing: muscle power "would never have been sufficient." The museum in Milan puts it plainly too: a full-size version could never have worked, mainly because of "the obvious lack of suitable motor."

**2. Even if it lifted, the frame would spin the other way.** This is the one most people miss, and it's my favorite. When you push something to turn it, it pushes back on you just as hard. If the people on the platform push the screw clockwise, the screw pushes the platform counterclockwise. On the ground, friction holds the platform still. The moment it leaves the ground, nothing does. Aerospace engineer J. Gordon Leishman puts it this way in *Principles of Helicopter Aerodynamics*: "a torque applied to the rotor shaft will result in a reaction torque tending to rotate the platform from which the torque is applied." He adds that Leonardo didn't seem to appreciate this. Modern helicopters solve it with a tail rotor, or with two rotors turning in opposite directions. Leonardo's drawing has neither.

You can feel this one too. Sit on a spinning office chair, lift your feet, hold something heavy out to the side, and swing it around. The chair turns the other way.

## Leonardo's own test

Here's the part I like most. Leonardo didn't just write "it will rise high" and move on. In the same note, he describes a way to test the idea small:

> "You may make a small model of pasteboard, of which the axis is formed of fine steel wire, bent by force, and as it is released it will turn the screw."

A cardboard screw, powered by a bent wire that springs back. No people, no platform. The museum in Milan reads this as Leonardo knowing a full-size version wouldn't work, so he suggested limiting the experiment to a small model. Whatever he thought, the method is right. If you're not sure an idea works, build the smallest version that can tell you, and watch what happens.

The notebook he drew it in is Manuscript B, folio 83 verso, now at the Bibliothèque de l'Institut de France in Paris. The library dates the notebook to around 1487–1489, from his years in Milan at the court of Ludovico Sforza, and describes it as especially rich in military engineering, architecture and mechanical flight.
