Before you read anything else, try this.

You have a square whose sides are 2 feet long, so its area is 4 square feet. You want a new square with exactly **double** the area: 8 square feet. How long should the new square's sides be?

Take a guess before you scroll. Pick a number.

If you said 4 feet, you're in good company. That's the first answer a boy gives Socrates in Plato's dialogue *Meno*, written more than 2,300 years ago (Plato died in 347 BCE). It's wrong, and the way he found out it was wrong is the reason this blog exists.

## 1. Socrates lets the boy be wrong first

In the *Meno*, Socrates draws the 2-foot square and asks a boy what side would make a figure twice as big. The boy answers right away: "Clearly, Socrates, double." Socrates turns to Meno and says: "Do you observe, Meno, that I am not teaching the boy anything, but merely asking him each time?" ([Meno 82e](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus:text:1999.01.0178:text=Meno:section=82e), trans. W.R.M. Lamb)

Then they check. A 4-foot side gives 4 × 4 = 16 square feet, which is four times the area, not two. So the boy tries again: three feet. That gives 3 × 3 = 9. Still not 8 ([83e](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus:text:1999.01.0178:text=Meno:section=83e)). Now he's stuck: "Well, on my word, Socrates, I for one do not know" ([84a](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus:text:1999.01.0178:text=Meno:section=84a)).

Most of us would count that as failure. Socrates counts it as progress. At first the boy "thought he knew... and confidently answered as though he knew." Now he "feels the difficulty he is in." Socrates asks whether "giving him the torpedo's shock" did him any harm. His answer is no: now the boy "will push on in the search gladly" ([84a–b](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus:text:1999.01.0178:text=Meno:section=84b)).

Here's the answer. Draw the diagonal of your original square, corner to corner, and build a new square on that line. The diagonal cuts the 2-foot square into two triangles of 2 square feet each. The square on the diagonal is made of four of those same triangles, so it has 4 × 2 = 8 square feet: exactly double. In Plato's version, the boy gets there himself, and Socrates names it: "the double space is the square of the diagonal" ([85b](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus:text:1999.01.0178:text=Meno:section=85b)). If you want the number, that side is √8, about 2.83 feet. That's why neither 3 nor 4 could ever work.

The point isn't the geometry trick. The boy only took in the right answer because he had already committed to a wrong one and watched it break.

## 2. Socrates on writing: "the appearance of wisdom"

Socrates had a complaint about a different information technology. In Plato's *Phaedrus*, he tells a story in which Theuth, the god who invented letters, shows them off to Thamus, king of Egypt ([274d](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus:text:1999.01.0174:text=Phaedrus:section=274d)). Writing, the king replies, "will produce forgetfulness in the minds of those who learn to use it, because they will not practice their memory." It is "an elixir not of memory, but of reminding," and it offers pupils "the appearance of wisdom, not true wisdom" ([Phaedrus 275a](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus:text:1999.01.0174:text=Phaedrus:section=275a), trans. H.N. Fowler).

It's a funny thing to read, because we only know it from writing. Socrates himself "never wrote anything" ([Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, "Socrates"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/socrates/)). His student Plato wrote the dialogues.

Writing obviously didn't ruin learning. But the worry is precise, and it fits a chatbot better than it fits a book. A system that hands you a polished answer to any question can give you the feeling of having learned something without you doing any of the work. In the *Meno* scene above, that would mean skipping straight to "the square of the diagonal": you'd have the answer, and none of the understanding that came from watching 4 and 3 fail.

## 3. Confucius shows one corner

Confucius was working on the same problem in a different civilization. In the *Analects*, the record of his sayings, he describes his rule plainly:

> "I do not open up the truth to one who is not eager to get knowledge, nor help out any one who is not anxious to explain himself. When I have presented one corner of a subject to any one, and he cannot from it learn the other three, I do not repeat my lesson." ([Analects VII.8](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/3330/pg3330.txt), trans. James Legge)

That's a teacher deliberately holding back. He gives you one corner and expects you to work out the other three. He also pairs it with the balance I think about most: "Learning without thought is labour lost; thought without learning is perilous" (Analects II.15, same translation). You need both input and your own effort. An answer machine supplies plenty of the first and very little of the second.

## 4. Aristotle: you learn a craft by doing it

Aristotle came to Athens to study at Plato's Academy from about age seventeen until Plato died. He later set up his own school in Athens, the Lyceum, where its members "conducted research into a wide range of subjects," from botany and biology to astronomy and ethics ([SEP, "Aristotle"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/aristotle/)).

In the *Nicomachean Ethics* he puts the learning-by-doing idea in one line: "We learn an art or craft by doing the things that we shall have to do when we have learnt it: for instance, men become builders by building houses, harpers by playing on the harp" ([NE 1103a](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus:text:1999.01.0054:bekker+page=1103a), trans. H. Rackham). You can't become a builder by reading about houses, and you don't learn geometry by reading that the answer is the diagonal.

## What these have in common

Put the four side by side:

- Socrates lets you guess wrong before you see the answer.
- Writing, by his account, risks the appearance of wisdom.
- Confucius shows one corner and waits for you to find the rest.
- Aristotle says you learn by doing the thing.

None of them is against information. All of them put the learner's own effort at the center: the guess, the struggle, the explanation in your own words. That's the part an answer machine is built to skip.
